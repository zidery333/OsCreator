# How Meta ads are actually run

## In one line
Pick one offer and one audience, run a direct sales or leads campaign, judge it on
what a customer is worth to you rather than on a ratio, and then leave it alone
long enough for Meta's algorithm to learn.

## What it says

### The structure you're working inside
Three levels, and every setting lives at one of them: **campaign** (the objective),
**ad set** (audience, placements, budget), **ad** (the creative people see). One
campaign can hold several ad sets; each ad set can hold several ads. `[04:38]`

When you launch or make a real change, Meta enters a **learning phase** — roughly
50 conversion events, typically 24–48 hours, but it is driven by conversion volume,
not by the clock. `[01:01]` `[03:04]` A campaign doing thousands of sales a day
exits in hours; one doing six leads a week can take a month.

### The method
1. **Set an optimisation schedule and stick to it.** Decide up front you'll adjust
   no more than once every 7–10 days. Every significant edit restarts the learning
   phase, so constant tinkering means you never leave it. `[03:34]` Small budgets
   need *longer* gaps, because fewer conversions means slower learning.
2. **Niche hard, deliberately.** With a small budget you cannot out-spend bigger
   competitors, so don't address their market. Pick the 1% you can serve better and
   write only to them. `[06:07]` If you already have customers, look at which ones
   repeat, stay longest and cause least trouble — target those. `[07:40]`
3. **Copy what's already working, from the Meta Ads Library.** It's free and public.
   Search a competitor you respect and look for ads **still running after ~6 months**
   — nobody keeps paying for an ad that loses money, so longevity is the proof.
   `[12:45]` `[13:47]` Model the format, not the words.
4. **Run direct-response only.** Sales or leads objectives. Not awareness, not
   traffic, not engagement. Meta is literal: ask for traffic and it finds people who
   click and don't buy. `[15:50]` `[17:21]`
5. **Consolidate.** One offer, one campaign, ideally one ad set. Five ad sets
   splitting 20 weekly conversions gives Meta four each and it learns from none of
   them. `[26:00]` `[26:30]`
6. **Test creative with organic first.** Post it to Reels; whatever outperforms
   organically, add a call to action and run it as an ad. Free testing you were
   doing anyway. `[17:52]` It doesn't always transfer, but the hook usually does.
7. **Lead with proof, not claims.** Around 80% of the ads in one practitioner's
   whole history are proof. `[06:10]` Proof strength is a continuum: an anonymous
   ten-year-old text testimonial at the weak end; at the strong end, someone
   visibly *like your customer*, live, saying what changed. Ask which version is
   hardest to fake — that's the one to use. `[07:11]`
8. **Own your own name in search, and retarget.** Bidding on your own brand terms
   effectively always makes money, and a pixel on the site turns traffic you're
   already getting into a second, cheaper audience. `[05:09]`

## The creative layer
Added 2026-08-30. The method above gets the account right; this is what decides
whether the ad works. Two agency practitioners, ~$1.45bn of tracked spend between
them.

**The hook is four things at once, in the first 3 seconds** — the text on screen,
the sound, the visual, and the overall vibe (lighting, font, feel). Most people
mean only the words when they say "hook". `[03:02]` Changing the *visual* hook
moves performance more than changing the words. `[03:34]`

**Hook patterns that repeatedly test well**, from ~5,000 ads in one year:
- *Investment* — the time or money someone spent failing before they found you.
  Mine your reviews for the failed attempts. `[00:30]` `[01:00]`
- *Trigger word* — "scam", "ruining my life". Works on loss aversion plus
  curiosity: "I thought this was a scam" leaves tension the brain must resolve. `[02:01]`
- *If you…* — makes people self-select into a group; shortcuts targeting. `[11:14]`
- *POV you hate X* — 10–15% of top performers contained "POV". `[06:09]`
- *Why did no one tell me* — implies withheld knowledge, positions authority. `[10:14]`
- *How to know if / how do I pick / how to start* — the same hook aimed at three
  different funnel stages. `[03:35]`
- *Founder introduces themselves as the founder* — widely advised against, and it
  keeps winning. Put the name and "founder" in the text overlay. `[07:10]` `[08:10]`

**Testing discipline:** 3–6 hook variations per creative test, and take big swings
rather than one-word changes. Reformat your existing top performers with new hooks
as a single large test. `[09:11]` `[09:43]`

**Production, in priority order:** speed beats perfection — the ad you spent least
time on often wins; clarity beats aesthetics — a beautiful ad nobody understands is
worthless; editing should amplify the feeling, not decorate it. `[15:50]` `[16:20]`
`[16:50]` Match format to funnel stage: UGC to introduce, longer video to convince. `[14:50]`

## Where they disagree
**What a beginner's first campaign should be.** One source says start with traffic
or engagement because they're simpler to understand `[07:10]`; the other says never
run those at all, go straight to leads or sales `[15:50]`. The disagreement is real
and it's about what the first campaign is *for* — learning the interface, or making
money. If money is the point, the second is the stronger argument, and it's the one
made by the source with the deeper track record.

**How far up the funnel to aim.** The small-budget argument is direct response
only — sales or leads, nothing else `[15:50]`. The creative strategists say Meta's
newer algorithm rewards content aimed at people *higher* up the funnel, and that
opening by stating the problem or showing the product is losing ground `[00:00]`.
Both are describing what they see; the difference is budget. With little money the
direct-response case holds, because you need the return to reinvest. At scale, the
top-of-funnel case is the one being tested by people spending millions.

## The judgement that separates them from beginners
**Decide what a customer is worth, then decide what you'll pay for one.** Beginners
fixate on a ratio — "I want a 10x ROAS". Practitioners work backwards from customer
value: a $2,000 customer makes a $400 acquisition cost obviously good. `[21:25]`
`[21:55]` ROAS *falls* as you scale and that is normal — 4x on $1M/month is a far
better business than 10x on $10k/month. `[23:57]` Several of the largest advertisers
in the world lose money on first purchase deliberately and earn it back over years.
`[24:28]`

## What goes wrong
- **Helicopter-parenting the campaign.** Daily fiddling, permanent learning phase. `[03:34]`
- **Too broad.** Most small-budget accounts are far too broad, not too niche. `[12:14]`
- **Refusing to pay enough per customer.** Caps the campaign below where it works. `[22:57]`
- **Splitting a small budget across many ad sets.** Nothing gets enough data. `[26:30]`
- **Over-stacking interests**, which shrinks the audience and raises cost. `[10:15]`
- **Forgetting the ad-account time zone**, so campaigns start or end a day out. `[12:50]`
- **A special-category offer** (credit, jobs, housing, politics, finance) not
  flagged as one — targeting is legally restricted there. `[10:46]`

## Practice
Open the Meta Ads Library, search three competitors, and list every ad still running
after six months. Write one sentence on what their hooks have in common. Twenty
minutes, costs nothing, and it's the input for your first creative.
**Pass:** you can name the shared pattern without looking back at the list.
